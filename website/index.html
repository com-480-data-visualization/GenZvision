How Gen Z Slang Lives and Dies on the Internet

One day nobody's heard of "skibidi." Three weeks later, your mom says it at dinner. We tracked 46 slang terms across 6 platforms, 22 regions, and over half a million posts to figure out exactly how internet language is born, goes viral, and quietly dies.

The Rise

Slang doesn't announce itself — it just suddenly appears everywhere at once. This heatmap captures the monthly heartbeat of the 15 most-used terms from 2020 to 2025. Each row is a word, each column is a month, and the brighter the cell, the more it was dominating the internet that month. Look at the streaks: some terms ignite instantly and burn out just as fast. Others smolder for years. But scroll to the right — by 2025, almost every row is dimming. Nothing stays hot forever.

Two patterns, one chart: "No cap" and "rent free" both peaked early and were effectively dead by 2023 — their entire lifespan fits in three rows. Meanwhile "sigma" and "brain rot" arrived late but are still the brightest cells on the right edge. The generation keeps refreshing its vocabulary. And notice the right side of the chart as a whole — even the survivors are cooling down. Gen Z slang isn't just trendy, it's temporary by design.
If nothing lasts forever, the real question is: who's ahead right now? That answer changes more often than you'd think.

The Race

Think of it as the most unstable leaderboard on the internet. Hit play and watch the top 10 terms compete in real time as cumulative usage stacks up month after month. The key thing to notice isn't who's winning — it's how rarely the order stays the same. Terms that own the top spot one month slip two places the next. A word you've barely heard of rockets in and immediately starts climbing. In five years of data, there is almost no month where the ranking looks exactly like the one before it.

The margins are brutal: In March 2024, the gap between #1 (Slay) and #10 (Mid) is less than 5,000 observations — across five years of cumulative data, that's almost nothing. One strong month is enough to flip three positions. Nobody is safe, nobody is locked out. The race never really settles.

The Spread

Here's the thing about slang: it never stays where it started. A word coined in a TikTok comment migrates to Twitter threads, gets picked up on Reddit, and eventually lands in Discord servers where it lives a second life. This Sankey diagram traces that journey — the left side shows where slang is born, the right side shows where it ends up being used. The width of each flow tells you how many terms traveled that exact path. The middle of the diagram is intentionally chaotic — and that's the point. Every platform sends slang to every other platform. None of it stays where it started. Hover over a flow to see the details.

The TikTok paradox: TikTok is the birthplace of 52% of all slang — but it only captures 40% of actual usage. It creates the language, then watches it leave. Meanwhile, Discord never originates slang but absorbs everything. It's a pure adoption platform — the place where slang goes to live after going viral somewhere else.
It's not that all slang rises and falls together. At any given moment, the whole lifecycle is happening at once.

Peak & Decline

Slang terms aren't just popular or unpopular — they're alive, and they age at different speeds. This bubble chart maps every tracked term by its current lifecycle phase: growing, peaking, declining, or dormant. Watch what happens as you move through time — the ecosystem never resets. While some terms are just being discovered, others are already burning out. There's always a new generation of words rising to replace whatever just peaked.

The ecosystem in motion: In September 2024, "Brat" and "Skibidi" are at their absolute peak while "Fanum Tax" and "Delulu" are already declining — and "Brain Rot" is still growing. Four different lifecycle stages, one moment in time. The internet doesn't wait for one trend to die before starting the next one.

The Vibes

You might expect slang to look completely different across contexts — one vocabulary for gaming, another for dating, another for political debates. And the terms do shift. But hover over any slice of this wheel and look at the outer bars — that's the age distribution. It barely changes. The same demographic mix shows up whether you're in a gaming lobby, a celebrity gossip thread, or a food review. The words adapt to the context. The people using them, not so much.

The uniform surprise: Look at the outer bars across any two sectors — they're almost identical. You'd expect gaming to skew heavily toward teenagers, or work contexts to trend older. But the age distribution stays remarkably consistent no matter the context. Slang hasn't just spread across platforms and terms — it's spread evenly across demographics too. Wherever you go, the crowd looks the same.
Before the vibes, before the spread — every word had a birthday.

The Chronicle

Every slang term was once brand new — and this timeline marks the exact moment each one entered the internet's vocabulary. Each dot is a birth. The color tells you which platform delivered it. Click any dot to see what was born that month. Notice the big cluster at the very start of 2020 — seven terms already in circulation when we began tracking. Those are the founding vocabulary of Gen Z internet culture. Everything after is what the algorithm added.

Red dominates: Scan the dots from left to right and one color keeps appearing — TikTok pink. The birthplace data backs up what The Spread already showed: most of what we call Gen Z slang was born in a TikTok comment section. The exceptions — the blue Twitter dots, the occasional Reddit orange — are the outliers that prove the rule.

The Cosmos

This is the emotional map of Gen Z slang. Every term is a star, positioned by two things: how positive or negative it feels (left to right) and how intensely people use it (bottom to top). Terms in the same category form constellations. But look closely — those constellations don't scatter randomly. They form vertical bands. Within any category, the sentiment barely changes from term to term. What varies is the intensity. The category locks in the emotional direction. The specific word just determines how hard people commit to it. Click any star to zoom into its galaxy and see all related terms in detail, or use the legend to filter by category.

Categories define the feeling: The insult constellation clusters tightly around -0.4 on the sentiment axis regardless of the specific term. Approval terms sit just as firmly at +0.4. Within each category, terms share nearly the same emotional valence — they just differ in intensity. When Gen Z reaches for a word, the type of word predetermines its emotional charge. The choice of specific term is about how much, not which direction.

The Map

Not everyone talks the same internet — and this map makes that visible. We tracked 12 states by slang usage volume and colored each one by how active its users are. The hotspots are immediately obvious. But here's the question worth asking: is this just population? More people, more posts? Or are some states genuinely punching above their weight as slang generators?

Both, but not equally: The top four states on this map — California, Texas, New York, Florida — are also the four most populous US states. Population explains the pattern at first glance. But California's dominance goes beyond its headcount: Texas has nearly the same population, yet CA's usage is significantly higher. Meanwhile, states like New Jersey and North Carolina — both with sizable populations — don't show up at all. Size gets you on the map. Culture gets you to the top of it.
Location shapes which slang you use. But age shapes whether you're allowed to.

The Generation Gap

Here's the unspoken rule of internet slang: the moment your parents start using it, it's already dead. But look at the overall distribution. Gen Z Core dominates at 32.2% — but Young Adults account for 21.3%, Millennials for 12.8%, and even Boomers have a slice. Combined, over a third of slang usage comes from outside the generation that created it. The rule is wrong, or at least incomplete. Select any term to watch how dramatically that shifts.

The common assumption doesn't hold up: Gen Z didn't lock the door behind them. Some terms do stay fiercely protected — skibidi barely registers above the under-18 crowd. But others like gaslighting have spread so far that they're no longer even slang, they're just vocabulary. The generation gap is real. It's just not a wall.
We've shown you the pattern. Now find the exceptions.

The Takeaway

52% of slang originates on TikTok
38% of tracked terms are already declining
6 mo average time from peak to decline

Gen Z slang looks chaotic from the outside. From the data, it's a system. Every term follows the same arc: born on TikTok, spreading cross-platform within weeks, peaking for a few months, then quietly replaced by whatever's next. The words change constantly. The pattern never does. And the pattern moves fast — by the time a slang term reaches mainstream media, Gen Z has usually already moved on. That's not a bug. That's the whole point.
