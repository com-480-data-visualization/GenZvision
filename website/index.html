How Gen Z Slang Lives and Dies on the Internet

One day nobody's heard of "skibidi." Three weeks later, your mom says it at dinner. We tracked 46 slang terms across 6 platforms, 22 regions, and over half a million posts to figure out exactly how internet language is born, goes viral, and quietly dies.

The Rise

Slang doesn't announce itself. One month "delulu" doesn't exist — the next, it's in every comment section on the internet. This heatmap captures the monthly heartbeat of the top 15 slang terms from 2020 to 2025. Each row is a word, each column is a month, and the brighter the cell, the harder it was trending. Look for the sudden bright streaks — those are the moments a term went from niche to unavoidable.

Look for: "Slay" lights up early and stays hot for years (19,000+ observations). But watch "delulu" — it goes from zero to everywhere in just a few months. That's the new pattern: faster rise, shorter reign.
Some terms flash and fade. Others stack up dominance for years. Who wins?

Think of it as the Olympics of internet slang. Hit play and watch the top 10 terms battle for the #1 spot in real-time as cumulative usage stacks up month after month. The leaderboard reshuffles constantly — early favorites get overtaken by newcomers, and a term you've never heard of can rocket to the top seemingly overnight. Watch for the upset: "Slay" dominates the early rounds like it owns the internet. But around mid-2023, "sigma" comes out of nowhere and starts an absurd climb. By 2024, "brain rot" joins the sprint. Gen Z doesn't just adopt slang — it constantly replaces it.

The Spread

Here's the thing about slang: it never stays where it started. A word coined in a TikTok comment migrates to Twitter threads, gets picked up on Reddit, and eventually lands in Discord servers where it lives a second life. This Sankey diagram traces that journey — the left side shows where slang is born, the right side shows where it ends up being used. The width of each flow tells you how many terms traveled that exact path. Hover over a flow to see the details.

The TikTok paradox: TikTok is the birthplace of 52% of all slang — but it only captures 40% of actual usage. It creates the language, then watches it leave. Meanwhile, Discord never originates slang but absorbs everything. It's a pure adoption platform — the place where slang goes to live after going viral somewhere else.
Every word has a shelf life. How long before the internet moves on?

Peak & Decline

Slang terms aren't just popular or unpopular — they're alive. Each one goes through a lifecycle: growing, peaking, declining, and eventually going dormant. This animated bubble chart lets you watch that process unfold in real time. Each bubble is a slang term — the bigger it is, the more it's being used that month. The color tells you its current phase. Hit play and watch entire vocabularies rise and fall over five years. Some terms burn bright and vanish. Others stubbornly refuse to die.

The brutal math: 38% of the terms we tracked are already in decline or dormant. Going viral is absurdly rare — only 0.1% of posts actually qualify — but when virality hits, it defines a term's entire peak. One viral TikTok can be the difference between "niche joke" and "cultural moment."

The Vibes

You don't use the same slang in a gaming lobby and a dating app (well, most people don't). This wheel maps 17 real-world contexts where slang actually shows up — from meme pages and fashion posts to food reviews and political debates. Vibes check: "Slay" absolutely owns fashion and compliments — it's the Swiss Army knife of positivity. The wheel also reveals something subtle: slang used in political contexts tends to skew more negative, while food and music contexts stay overwhelmingly positive.

The Chronicle

Every slang term was once brand new. "Bussin" showed up in early 2020 when nobody was watching. "Demure" didn't exist until late 2024. This timeline maps the exact moment each word entered the internet's vocabulary — click any dot to see what was born that month, which platform it came from, and how many people eventually picked it up. The dot color tells you the origin platform; the size tells you how many terms appeared at once.

The 2023 explosion: Something wild happened in 2023 — the internet birthed 11 new slang terms in a single year, including "skibidi," "brain rot," and "sigma." That's more than any other year in our dataset. TikTok was the delivery room for most of them. 2024 slowed down but got weirder: "demure" and "mindful" appeared simultaneously, which somehow makes perfect sense.

The Cosmos

Welcome to the emotional universe of Gen Z slang. Every term is a star, floating in a space defined by two axes: how positive or negative the word feels (left to right) and how intensely people use it (bottom to top). Stars that belong to the same category form galaxies — clusters of related terms that share a vibe. The bigger the star, the more widely used the term. Click any star to zoom into its galaxy and see all related terms in detail, or use the legend to filter by category. Hover a star to read its full emotional profile.

The surprise: Sentiment and intensity are almost completely uncorrelated (r = 0.06). That means Gen Z uses negative words just as passionately as positive ones. "Brat" is mildly negative but deployed with extreme intensity. "Slay" is positive but used casually. The emotional weight of slang has nothing to do with whether the word is "good" or "bad" — it's all about how hard people commit to it.

The Map

Not everyone talks the same internet. Slang adoption varies wildly across the United States — and the map reveals some fascinating regional personalities. We tracked 12 states and colored each one by how actively its users adopt slang. The darker the shade, the more slang is in active circulation. Hover over any state to see its favorite term, top platform, and overall sentiment score.

The coastal divide: California alone generates nearly 20% of all US slang usage — it's not just a trendsetter in fashion, it's a linguistic powerhouse. New York follows close behind. Meanwhile, the South and Midwest march to a different beat: "based" is the dominant term there, while "slay" rules the coasts. Same language, different vocabularies.
The moment your parents start saying it, it's already dead. But is that actually true?

The Generation Gap

Here's the unspoken rule of internet slang: it belongs to whoever discovers it first — and it dies the moment it crosses a generational boundary. But the data tells a more nuanced story. Some terms like "skibidi" are almost exclusively teen property (42% of usage from 13-17 year olds). Others like "gaslighting" have spread so wide that grandparents use them in Facebook comments. Select a term below and watch the donut reshape itself — each arc represents an age group, and the orbiting particles show you the crowd behind the numbers.

The generational filter: Terms fall into clear profiles. "Teen-dominated" words (skibidi, sigma, rizz) live and die by the under-18 crowd. "Core Gen Z" terms (slay, based, bussin) peak at 18-24. But the most interesting category is "multi-generational" — words like gaslighting and toxic that transcended internet culture and became actual vocabulary. Those never die.

The Takeaway

52% of slang originates on TikTok
38% of tracked terms are already declining
6 mo average time from peak to decline

Gen Z slang isn't random noise — it follows a predictable pattern. Born on TikTok, adopted across platforms within weeks, peaking in months, and fading just as fast. The specific words change constantly, but the lifecycle never does: a term explodes, spreads unevenly across regions and platforms, burns bright for a few months, and then quietly gets replaced by whatever comes next. By the time mainstream media writes about a slang term, Gen Z has usually already moved on.
