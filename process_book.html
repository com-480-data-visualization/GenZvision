1. Overview

GenZvision is an interactive scrollytelling website that tells the story of how Gen Z slang evolves across the internet. We tracked 46 slang terms across 6 platforms, 22 regions, and more than 535,000 observations from 2020 to 2025. The goal was to go beyond a simple dashboard and build something that actually feels like reading a story — one where you scroll through chapters, each revealing a different angle on how internet language is born, spreads, peaks, and dies.

The project started in Milestone 1 with a Kaggle dataset and an idea. By Milestone 2 we had a working prototype with four visualizations. For Milestone 3, we rebuilt almost everything and ended up with 11 full sections, each with its own D3.js visualization, narrative text, and interactive elements.

2. From Sketches to Final Product

We drew our initial sketches by hand during Milestone 1 to figure out which visualization types could answer our core question: how does Gen Z slang evolve and spread? Below we show the original sketches alongside what the final visualizations became, and explain why things changed along the way.

1 Timeline Heatmap (The Rise)

Sketch: A standard heatmap with rows = terms, columns = months, and a YlOrRd color scale for usage intensity.

Final: Stayed very close to the sketch. We kept the heatmap format because it works — you can immediately spot when a term peaked and for how long. The main difference is that we added smooth hover tooltips that show the exact month, count, and sentiment. We also chose to limit it to the top 15 terms instead of all 46, because showing everything at once made it unreadable. The YlOrRd scale was swapped for a custom purple-based palette to match our overall dark theme.

2 Platform Sankey (The Spread)

Sketch: Origin platforms on the left, usage platforms on the right, flow width proportional to number of terms.

Final: Almost identical to the sketch. The Sankey was one of the first visualizations we got working and it barely changed. We added flow highlighting on hover (the selected flow stays bright while others fade) and a detail tooltip. The biggest insight this reveals — TikTok originates 52% of slang but only captures 40% of usage — became a central talking point in our narrative.

3 Bubble Chart (Peak & Decline)

Sketch: Bubbles = terms, color = lifecycle phase, size = monthly usage. Animated over time.

Final: The concept is the same but the implementation was more complex than we expected. Getting the force simulation to feel natural while also encoding lifecycle phase in color took a lot of tuning. We added a play/pause button and a time slider so people can control the animation. The tooltip shows the term meaning, origin platform, and current phase. The four lifecycle colors (green = growing, red = peak, orange = declining, gray = dormant) work really well as a visual summary of where each term stands at any given month.

4 Bar Chart Race (The Race)

Sketch: Ranked lanes where terms overtake each other over time, like a sports leaderboard.

Final: This was originally a stretch goal but we implemented it early because it's such a fun way to show temporal competition. The main departure from the sketch is that we used horizontal bars instead of "lanes," which ended up looking cleaner. Watching "sigma" overtake "slay" around mid-2023 is genuinely satisfying and always gets a reaction when we show it to people.

5 The Slang Cosmos

Sketch: A scatter plot with X = sentiment, Y = intensity, terms connected by category into "constellations" on a dark background.

Final: This one changed the most from the sketch. We started with a simple constellation-style scatter plot, but it felt too static and not visually striking enough. We ended up building a full galaxy-themed background with actual spiral galaxy formations at each constellation centroid. Each galaxy has parametric spiral arms generated mathematically, a glowing core, and its size is proportional to how many terms belong to that category. We also added 350 twinkling background stars, a Milky Way band of micro-particles, and cross-shaped spikes on the larger data points. The result is much more ambitious than the original sketch, but it preserves the core data encoding: position maps sentiment vs. intensity, and the key insight (r = 0.06 correlation) still comes through.

6 US Regional Map (The Map)

Sketch: A US choropleth colored by slang adoption intensity.

Final: Close to the sketch, but we added a click-to-explore panel. When you click a state, a side panel shows its top terms as a bar chart, the dominant platform, and average sentiment. We used a purple monochrome gradient (light lavender to deep violet) so the color scale has a logical progression. The original plan only covered the US map, but we ended up adding a full world map in the Explore section too, which uses the same click-to-explore pattern with country-level data.

Visualizations We Added Beyond the Original Plan

Several visualizations were not in the M1/M2 sketches at all. They came from questions that naturally arose as we built the story:

The Vibes (Vibe Wheel)

We realized that where slang is used (gaming, dating, memes, food reviews...) matters as much as when and where geographically. The Vibe Wheel is a radial chart with 17 context slices, each containing the dominant terms for that context. Inner rings show sentiment, outer rings show age distribution. This replaced the originally planned ridgeline plot, which only showed sentiment distributions — the wheel shows more dimensions in a more compact and visually interesting way.

The Chronicle

A horizontal timeline showing the exact month each slang term was first coined. Dot size = number of terms born that month, dot color = origin platform. This gives a clear answer to "when were all these words invented?" and reveals that 2023 was an explosion year with 11 new terms.

The Generation Gap

This came from asking "does your age determine your slang?" We built an animated donut chart with 200 orbiting particles that redistribute by age group when you switch terms. Selecting "skibidi" shows 42% teen usage with pink particles dominating the orbit. Selecting "gaslighting" shows an even spread with a "multi-generational" badge. The donut arc transitions use D3 arc interpolation for smooth morphing.

Term Explorer

A searchable card-based explorer where you can look up any term's full biography: usage curve, peak month, top platform, sentiment, and lifecycle phase. This is the "details on demand" piece that closes the Shneiderman mantra (overview first, zoom and filter, then details).

3. Design Decisions

Visual Identity

We made a deliberate choice to go with a dark theme (near-black background with purple/pink accents) rather than a white academic dashboard look. The reasoning: our audience is students and young adults interested in internet culture, and the topic is Gen Z slang. A bright white layout would feel out of place. The dark aesthetic with glowing accents matches the "internet at night" feel of platforms like TikTok and Discord where this slang actually lives.

Color choices were not random. We used the Viridis palette for sequential data (heatmaps), a categorical 5-hue scheme (pink, purple, blue, green, gold) for age groups in the Generation Gap, and a monochrome purple gradient for the geographic maps. Every color choice was tested against the dark background for contrast and readability.

Scrollytelling Structure

We chose scrollytelling over a tab-based dashboard because the data has a natural narrative arc. There's a logical order: first you see when terms appear (The Rise), then how fast they spread (The Race), then where they go (The Spread), then how they age (Peak & Decline), then deeper dives into context, sentiment, geography, and demographics. A dashboard would lose that progression.

Between each section, we added short transition sentences ("Some terms flash and fade. Others stack up dominance for years. Who wins?") that connect the narrative and guide the reader to the next question. This follows the L12 storytelling principle of maintaining a linear flow with clear transitions.

Lazy Rendering

Every visualization is rendered only when it first scrolls into view, using the Intersection Observer API. This was both a performance decision (rendering 11 D3 visualizations on page load would be slow) and a storytelling one (the user discovers each chart as they scroll, matching the narrative reveal).

Interaction Philosophy

We followed a "hover reveals, click explores" pattern everywhere. Hovering any data point shows a tooltip with details. Clicking (where supported) opens a deeper view — a side panel on the maps, a full term profile in the explorer. Animated sections (Race, Bubbles) have explicit play/pause controls so the user stays in control. We avoided auto-playing animations that the user can't stop, which felt disrespectful of their attention.

4. Technical Challenges

Force Simulation Tuning

The hero word cloud and the bubble chart both use D3 force simulation. Getting bubbles to not overlap while still looking organic (not grid-like) required a lot of parameter tweaking. We ended up using a combination of forceCenter, forceManyBody (with negative strength for repulsion), forceCollide (sized to each bubble), and forceX/forceY to keep things loosely centered. The alpha decay was set to 0.05 so the simulation settles quickly.

Galaxy Generation for Cosmos

The biggest technical challenge was making the Cosmos galaxies look realistic. Our first attempt used simple radial blobs, but they looked like generic circles. We rewrote the generator to use parametric logarithmic spiral equations: each arm follows theta = armOffset + t * 3.2, with particles placed along the arm at increasing distances. The spiral is then flattened into an ellipse shape (compressed Y by 0.6) and rotated by a random angle per galaxy. This produces convincing spiral arm structures.

Arc Interpolation for Age Gap

When switching between terms in the Generation Gap donut chart, the arcs need to smoothly morph from one size to another. D3 doesn't do this automatically for arc paths. We implemented custom attrTween functions that interpolate between the previous and next arc datum, storing the previous state on each DOM element (this._prev = d). This gives a smooth, continuous transition instead of a jarring jump.

Map Color Visibility

Getting the world map colors right took several iterations. The first attempt used dark purple that blended into the black background. We tried teal-to-violet gradients but that looked illogical ("why are low-usage countries a different color family?"). We settled on a monochrome purple gradient from light lavender (#b8a9d4) to deep violet (#7c3aed) with a power curve (0.65 exponent) to spread the middle values more evenly.

Responsive Layout in Two-Column Sections

Most sections use a two-column grid (text left, viz right). This works well on desktop but breaks on narrow screens. We added CSS breakpoints at 900px to stack columns vertically. Some visualizations (the Chronicle timeline, the Generation Gap donut) needed their own custom layouts because the two-column format was too constraining — the donut was only 337px wide and the labels got clipped. We switched those sections to a centered, stacked layout.

5. Data Pipeline

The original Kaggle dataset (535,396 rows, 22 columns) was too large and too flat for direct visualization. We wrote Python preprocessing scripts to transform it into 9 focused JSON files, each optimized for a specific visualization:

JSON FileWhat It ContainsUsed By
slang_timeline.jsonMonthly usage counts per term (2020-2025)Heatmap, Race
platform_flow.jsonOrigin platform → usage platform flow countsSankey
slang_lifecycle.jsonMonthly lifecycle phase per termBubble chart
top_terms_summary.jsonAggregated stats per term (total usage, sentiment, top platform)Hero, Explorer
vibe_wheel_data.jsonContext × term matrix with sentiment and age dataVibe Wheel
fresco_data.jsonBirth date, origin platform, and first-month stats per termChronicle
slang_cosmos.jsonSentiment score, intensity, category, and usage per termCosmos
regional_data.jsonUsage by US state and world region with top termsMaps
age_data.jsonAge group distributions (5 groups) per term with profilesGeneration Gap

Each file is small enough (under 100KB) to load instantly over the network. The preprocessing scripts aggregate, pivot, and filter the raw data so the JavaScript code can focus on rendering rather than computation.

The age_data.json file required a more creative approach: the raw dataset doesn't have clean age-per-term breakdowns, so we derived age profiles from the aggregated usage patterns. We identified 5 archetypal profiles (teen-heavy, core Gen Z, young adult, wide, older-skew) and assigned each term to a profile based on its category and usage patterns, then generated realistic distributions from those profiles.

What Went Right

  • Starting with the narrative: Deciding on the story structure early (Rise → Race → Spread → Decline → ...) made it clear what each visualization needed to show. We never built a chart "just because" — each one answers a specific question in the narrative.
  • Modular architecture: Each visualization lives in its own JS file with a single render function. This made parallel development easy — one person could work on the Cosmos while another worked on the Vibe Wheel without merge conflicts.
  • Dark theme commitment: Choosing the dark aesthetic early and sticking with it gave the whole site a cohesive look. The glow effects, particle animations, and galaxy background all feel natural on a dark canvas.

What We Would Do Differently

  • Mobile experience: The site works on mobile but some visualizations (especially the heatmap and Sankey) are cramped on small screens. We would invest more in mobile-specific layouts or simplified mobile views.
  • Accessibility: Our color choices prioritize aesthetics over accessibility. We should have tested with colorblind simulation tools and added ARIA labels to interactive elements.
  • Data validation: The age group data was derived from profiles rather than raw observations. With more time, we'd find a cleaner data source for generational breakdowns or run a validation study.
  • Performance testing: On very slow connections, loading 9 JSON files sequentially can cause delays. We would bundle the data or add loading states per section.

10. Conclusion

GenZvision started as a simple idea — let's visualize how internet slang works — and grew into a full scrollytelling experience with 11 interactive D3.js visualizations. The journey from hand-drawn sketches to animated galaxy backgrounds and orbiting particle systems was longer than we expected, but the result is something we're genuinely proud of.

The core insight we wanted people to take away is that slang isn't random noise. It follows a predictable pattern: born on TikTok, adopted across platforms within weeks, peaking in months, and fading just as fast. The specific words change constantly, but the lifecycle never does.

Building this project taught us that data visualization isn't just about making charts — it's about choosing the right story to tell and then finding the right visual encoding for each part of that story. A heatmap answers "when," a Sankey answers "where to," a donut chart answers "who." Each visualization earned its place by answering a question the previous one raised.

If we had to summarize what we learned in one sentence: the best visualizations don't just show data, they make you curious about what comes next.
